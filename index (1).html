01 / The challenge

Helpful assistant—or shortcut machine?

The scenario describes a popular AI app that adapts quizzes, explains concepts, tracks weak areas and answers doubts instantly. Educators worry that it could weaken critical thinking, group learning and deep understanding.

What the app promises

Personalised practice, quick explanations, targeted revision and help when a teacher is unavailable. These are potential benefits—not proof of improved learning.

What needs protecting

Independent reasoning, peer discussion, fair access, accurate explanations and student privacy. Popularity or time spent in an app cannot prove understanding.

The decision: Should the college encourage students to use the app? Our recommendation is a limited, voluntary and supervised four-week trial—not an immediate full rollout or a complete ban.
02 / Six thinking hats

Change the lens. Change the conversation.

Select a hat to see the question it asks and how it applies to the AI exam-prep case.

White Hat — Establish the facts

We know which features the case says the app offers, but we have no independent trial results. Faculty should check answer accuracy, syllabus fit, privacy terms, accessibility and whether students can solve new problems without AI.

03 / Responsible study loop

Think First, AI Next

AI should support the effort of learning—not replace it. Use this five-step loop for each practice question.

Attempt

Try the question yourself first. An imperfect attempt is useful evidence.

Ask for a hint

Request a clue or guiding question before viewing a full solution.

Explain

Close the answer and explain the idea in your own words.

Discuss

Compare methods, questions and mistakes with classmates.

Check

Solve a fresh, similar problem without AI to test understanding.

04 / Proposed solution

Start small. Measure what matters.

A reversible pilot lets the college gather evidence before deciding whether to continue.

Primary plan

Four-week voluntary pilot

  • Begin with one subject and a no-AI baseline task.
  • Use attempt → hint → explain → discuss → check.
  • Hold a weekly peer-learning session.
  • Have faculty review sample answers and report errors.
  • Explain data collection clearly and offer an equivalent non-app route.
Backup plan

Keep learning moving

  • If privacy, accuracy or access requirements fail, pause or stop the app.
  • Use teacher-reviewed quizzes and staged hint sheets.
  • Run peer-study circles and scheduled doubt-clearing sessions.
  • Resume only if serious concerns are resolved.

The conclusion

Use AI as a practice partner—not a replacement for thinking.

Continue only if evidence shows students can understand, explain and apply concepts independently, with fair access and appropriate safeguards.
